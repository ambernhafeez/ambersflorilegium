# The Jinni to the Fisherman

<cite>From The Story of the Fisherman and the Jinni in The Annotated Arabian Nights: Tales From 1001 Nights, translated by Yasmine Seele</cite>
</blockquote>

There is no better way to set off a chain reaction of storytelling than a gambit like this one. This is just one of countless tales in the 1001 Nights where characters try to manipulate and school each other through the use of stories - all of which are really stories told to King Shahriyar, whom Shahrazad is trying to mellow in order to keep her head.

Each story adds subtle layers that form a kaleidoscopic patina as you read on and on. I have a lot more to say about the 1001 Nights in the future, but for now... 

# On Repetition and Discovery

I'm writing a short story about an early-career scientist who repeats the same experiment over and over again, fails, but learns something about themselves, the scientific process and the experiment each time. This story has a typical nested story structure, with sciencey backdrops and costumes. Equally, science is as typical of the human experience as anything else, because anything can be experiment and research. In both cases, people are not as objective about their progress and the interpretation of their results as they should be. We all hope for a certain outcome when we start something, but cannot be guaranteed a certain result; to go in expecting the exact result you planned for either results in 'failure' or introduces bias - which I would argue is the real failure.

I'm trying to look at my own writing more objectively, at each draft as a replicate. A few weeks ago, I woke up and 'realised' that I had to bin the entire act 1 of my novel, because something about the timeline was a bit off and now nothing made sense and the characters were not at the right point in their development. I'd wasted all that time writing a rubbish act 1, and I was a rubbish writer who could never write a novel, etc. I then had a few coffees and read my book and went for a walk and realised that actually, it's fine. I could shift the scenes I'd written to later on and write the black hole version of them in their place, which would hopefully make those scenes more impactful when the time comes for them. I'd jumped the gun because I was excited to write those scenes, because they were important to the character and based on memories of mine that I wanted to explore. 

The scenes weren't wrong, they were a stepping stone towards what I actually needed to write. They were the first replicate of that part of my novel, the trial experiment; I'd realised that something in my protocol had gone wrong, so those results couldn't answer the question: 'What is this character doing right now in act 1?'. I'd answered a different question: 'What activities are central to who my character is? What might she be doing if she had the choice?'. 

So yes, I needed to repeat my experiment, and it's frustrating. I'd love to have the first part of my novel settled up, so I could move on and continue to torture my characters in new and unusual ways. What I have instead is a few scenes from later on in my novel and the beginnings of the next replicate, written with tighter parameters in place. 

Another result of the failure of my first replicate was the idea for the short story I mentioned in the beginning, which I had while on my walk, once I'd stopped catastrophising about my novel. I was listening to an audiobook of Spring Summer Asteroid Bird by Henry Lien and was reminded of how much I love nested story structures, how important repetition is to uncovering deeply buried truths. I have a bit of a 1001 Nights obsession, and I've also recently been reading The Sandman: World's End, which happens to incorporate nested stories. It feels like everything is connected, and I want to ride that wave of ideas. In writing as in so much else, you are what you eat!

I don't know why it's so hard to apply what I know from science to my creative endeavours - that failure is an everyday experience, that success only comes from the boring, grunt work of repetition: try, adjust, try again, repeat endlessly until you have enough good data points, then go through the same iterative process to interpret the results in a meaningful way. This is very analogous to the writing and editing process. I wouldn't have high hopes for my first try at a new lab protocol, but writing a new scene feels very urgent, as if I only have one try to get it right and if it comes out wrong I'm a terrible writer and terrible person. It's probably because writing is more exposing emotionally. Scientific failure hurts, too, but usually not until you've failed at something three or more times. The first few tries aren't something to take personally.

Another challenge I'm trying to overcome right now is the transition from short to long-form writing. I started creative writing with poetry, then flash fiction, short fiction and now I am embarking on my first novel. Before this, my longest piece was about six thousand words. Six thousand to sixty thousand is a big jump, and requires a very different approach. With shorter pieces, especially flash pieces under a thousand words, I would usually form an idea, picture it perfectly, come up with the first few lines or sentences, and at that point I knew that when I sat down to write, I would churn out the whole piece exactly as I'd envisioned it. There's of course editing and revisions, but the major work is done quite quickly. The longer my projects get, the more I am having to rely on other methods of progress; I can't condense a whole novel into one moment, I can't visualise it, and so I have to explore and try and fail and try again. I still sometimes have those moments of inspiration for individual scenes, but the ideas don't come in a convenient order and might not gel with the character development or other factors at that point in the novel; there's a lot more context to consider for each component.

So, my resolution is to feel better about starting again, because I'm not starting from scratch; each try has taught me something, even if the word count goes back to 0. And no matter what I write in each session, I'm inevitably going to rewrite and edit it a few times later on, so I'm going to move forward being less of a perfectionist. Even my handling of that little jaunt into catastrophising territory a few weeks ago was, for me, a success - a few years ago, I was completely defenceless against bad feelings once they started. Nowadays, I can lose hours to anxious thoughts, but not (usually) days or weeks, because I've iterated the process of getting a handle on things so many times that I'm now better at it (one of the perks of getting older...).

At the longer-term scale, I'm hopefully going to learn from each attempt at writing a novel, too. I'll probably look back at this first attempt and laugh, or cringe horribly.

TLDR; I'm no longer expecting myself to get it right the first time, and I hope you won't either!
